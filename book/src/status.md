# Development status

Statuses are **done**, **partial**, or **open**. Every *partial* capability is broken down
further — what exists and what specifically remains — in the developer chapter,
[Implementation status in detail](developers/status-detail.md), so the work can be picked up
without re-deriving it from the code. This page is the evaluator's view: what the appliance does
today, what it does not, and where the project is heading.

One reorientation frames everything below. The design chapters now describe a **two-component
product**: an appliance with no management surface of its own, dialing home over one
mutually-authenticated channel, and a central [management server](design/management-server.md) that
owns onboarding, configuration and telemetry. Very little of that exists yet — the
[redesign table](#management-plane-redesign) below records it as open — while the appliance built so
far, its plain-HTTP management surface included, is present, working, and deliberately kept until
the channel replaces it: the end-to-end gate's whole cross-check strategy rests on those HTTP
surfaces, so they are erased last, not first.

## The current deployable system

The current deployable system is a two-dataplane-port **filtering** IPv4 slice: one driver
protection domain per port brings up a `virtio-net-pci` device on QEMU q35 from static seL4
capabilities alone, and an isolated forwarder protection domain decides each frame between the two
ports — parsing it, working out where it would go, consulting the operator's filter rules, and
rewriting its Ethernet and IPv4 headers in place if a rule permits it. A frame is never moved **between** buffers: one pool buffer carries it from one
NIC's DMA to the other's, and only the 34 rewritten header bytes travel back into it. The decision
itself is taken on a private copy, because a verdict reached on bytes a peer may rewrite underneath
it is no verdict — so a hop costs one copy of the payload, and a second when the recording tap is
attached.

That configuration is a schema-validated XML document, read and committed by a protection domain
of its own that holds no device and no dataplane memory, and handed to the forwarder through a
shared region under an offer/acknowledge protocol. The forwarder boots **fail-closed** — an empty
table, forwarding nothing — and switches to a configuration only after re-deciding, itself, every
one of the 42 rules the validating domain applied — at that domain's own strength on all but two,
each named where it is declared and each about something the image cannot see or has no stake in — so
a compromised parser cannot hand it a table those rules refuse. Every boot therefore performs a live
configuration swap on a running dataplane.

A document can now be **submitted to a running node** over the management API: `POST /config` takes
an XML document, `GET /config` states the one in force, and a submission that passes every rule is
committed under the next generation and picked up by the dataplane at its next poll boundary. The
end-to-end gate proves the whole of that on the release image — it boots a node, injects traffic the
shipped policy forwards, `POST`s a document that reverses that policy, waits for the forwarding
domain to report the new generation, and injects again: the traffic that was forwarded is now dropped
and the traffic that was dropped is now forwarded, with no domain having restarted in between. Two
documents submitted after it are refused with a reason and leave the generation where it was: one the
reader stops, and one that parses cleanly and a *rule* refuses — the case where a configuration could
half-apply at all, so the node is additionally held to still stating what it committed and still
deciding traffic by it. A separate boot shows the other end of the same posture: a node whose *own*
document a rule refuses comes up on generation 0, forwards nothing at all, and says so on its serial
console, which is the only surface it has. The document a build embeds is now the *first* generation
rather than the only one; a **hardware** change still requires a new image, which is the line the
design draws.

**Anyone who can reach the management port can replace this appliance's policy.** There is no
authentication and no TLS in front of it — see the paragraph below — so the port must not be exposed
to an untrusted network.

A **dedicated management port** is the third NIC, and it is an addressed IPv4 endpoint that
answers for itself and forwards nothing. It answers ARP requests for its own address, ICMP echo
requests to it, and HTTP over a first-party TCP stack: `GET /metrics` returns a Prometheus
exposition, and `GET /logs.pcapng` and `GET /capture.pcapng` return the two traffic recordings
whole; `GET /config` states the running configuration and `POST /config` replaces it. All of it is
plain HTTP — **there is no TLS and no authentication in front of any of it yet**, so anyone who can
reach the port can scrape the metrics, download every packet the appliance recorded, read the policy,
and *replace* it. That last one is not a lesser gap than the others: it is the authority to decide
what this firewall forwards. Until the [management-plane redesign](design/management.md) replaces
this surface with the onboarding flow and the authenticated channel, the management port belongs on
an isolated network and nowhere else. The port's MAC, address and prefix
come from the configuration document like every other address on the appliance, so the port is
configured rather than compiled in.

The isolation between management and dataplane is a property of what each domain is granted, not a
rule anybody has to remember: the management domain holds no dataplane memory and the forwarder
holds no management memory, and the end-to-end gate asserts the same exclusion on the wire, in
both directions, on the release image — no frame injected on the management port ever appears on a
dataplane port, and no dataplane probe ever appears on the management one.

Another protection domain owns the serial console — the PC-compatible COM1 port — and is the only
writer of the line; every other domain reaches an operator by publishing a typed record, which the
console domain renders and puts on the line. It replaced a kernel debug facility the release
kernel is not built with: until this landed, a **release image printed nothing at all**, so a node
that parked on a refused NIC or came up fail-closed on a refused document said so only in the
profile nobody ships.

Booting that release image for the first time found a second, deeper defect underneath the first,
which had been latent since the boot chain was written: the bootloader was free to place the
system image in the 640 KiB below 1 MiB, from where seL4's x86 boot would load the userland image
over its own running kernel — a triple fault before any protection domain executed. Whether it
happened was decided by whether the system image happened to fit down there, so **the debug
profile was green by luck**. The GRUB configuration now denies the bootloader that memory and the
build refuses an image small enough to fit what remains; see
[Signed boot chain](developers/status-detail.md#signed-boot-chain).

Both defects were reachable only in the configuration no gate booted, and both are the reason the
gate now boots the shipped one: every QEMU scenario in `make ci` runs the release image, and the
only debug kernel any gate boots is the one re-run to diagnose a scenario that has already failed.

A clock domain establishes what time it is. It calibrates the timestamp counter against the HPET —
a timer whose rate is self-describing — reads the real-time clock once for an epoch to anchor that
counter to, and publishes the calibration to every other domain. **Every structured record
therefore carries the UTC instant it was emitted at**, rendered as RFC 3339 — and the records
emitted before the calibration exists carry the token `unsynchronized` rather than a fake 1970. It
is **not** a trusted time source — see the status table below and its
[detail](developers/status-detail.md#trusted-time-source).

That domain is also the appliance's **only source of periodic wakeups**. Nothing else here is
entered by the passage of time: a domain is woken by a frame or by a peer, so one holding a deadline
on a silent link would wait at it for ever — and that is exactly the situation the management
channel's reconnection backoff exists for. So the clock domain arms one of the timer's comparators
and hands each interrupt on to the domain that dials, which is what lets a schedule advance on time
rather than on traffic.

A recorder domain owns the appliance's **block device** and turns the traffic into a durable
record. It brings a virtio-blk device up, proves the path to the medium by reading a sector and
writing a recognisable one back, and then writes **two pcapng recordings** onto that device, and
they differ by *what they record*. The **capture** holds every frame the pipeline reached a verdict
about, with that verdict on it: allow or deny, the reason, the rule where one matched, and the
conversation it belongs to. The **connection history** holds a record only where the appliance
reached a lifecycle or policy event — a conversation opened, advanced or closed, a policy refusal, a
tracker refusal — anchored to the packet that caused it and carrying that packet's L2–L4 headers.
Both are lifted while the frame is still the one that arrived rather than the one this appliance will
send on, which is what makes a recording evidence about the wire and not about the appliance's own
output. Either recording is downloadable whole over the management port — `GET /logs.pcapng`,
`GET /capture.pcapng` — and `tcpdump -r` opens both natively. Each recording is a ring across a
fixed extent of the disk, and the first sectors of that extent are its **superblock**: a doubled,
checksummed record of the ring's geometry and of how far it has been durably written, so the extent
can be read back from the medium alone with nothing else to consult. The recorder is the only
domain in the system that can put a byte on persistent storage, and the only path between it and
the dataplane is a one-way tap that can never backpressure forwarding.

**The appliance filters.** A packet is forwarded because a rule the operator wrote permits it, and
for no other reason: the `<rules>` section of the configuration document is a list of stateless
L2–L4 rules, matched first-match-wins in document order, and a packet no rule is about is
dropped. That last part is the whole posture and it is not a setting — the default deny is a
property of the fallthrough rather than of any document, so there is no `<rules>` section an
operator can write that makes an unmatched packet pass, and an empty one forwards nothing. Each
rule matches on ingress and egress interface, source and destination CIDR block, protocol, source
and destination port or port range, and ICMP type; every criterion is written out, `any` included,
so no attribute widens a rule by being left out. The two refusals a filter can reach stay separate
findings — a rule that said drop, and the fallthrough — and each rule carries its own hit counter on
`/metrics` under the id the document gave it.

**The filter is stateful, and it follows pf's model rather than netfilter's.** A connection tracker
sits between the forwarding decision and the filter, and a packet an existing flow already accounts
for is forwarded without the filter being consulted at all. So a ruleset decides which conversations
may **open**, and the traffic that follows one is carried by the flow. A reply comes back with no
rule naming it, which is the whole value of tracking state — the alternative is writing the reverse
of every rule and opening the appliance in both directions to permit one. And an edit to the policy
cannot cut a conversation already running *on the packet path*, because the rule that admitted it was
consulted once, when it opened. Under netfilter's model that acceptance is a rule an operator writes and can forget;
here it is structural, which is the trade this appliance makes for the OT environments it is aimed
at.

**And a commit can still end a conversation, because it re-decides the table rather than the
packet.** The moment a configuration commits, the appliance sweeps its own flow table against the new
policy and takes back every conversation that policy would no longer admit — once per commit rather
than once per packet, so the ruleset stays off the hot path and every flow the new policy still allows
is left exactly as it was. A host found to be compromised can therefore be cut off by an edit to the
document, which is what the model owed and did not previously deliver.

**How long that takes does not depend on how many conversations there are.** A pass is carried across
wakeups, and how much of it a wakeup works off scales with how full the table is — so a million-flow
table an attacker filled is swept in the same number of wakeups an empty one is, rather than sixteen
times as many. On the million-slot table this appliance builds that is at most 513 wakeups per pass at
any occupancy, and a commit arriving mid-pass queues one fresh pass behind the running one rather than
restarting it — so at most two passes, however fast documents are submitted. What that costs and the
arithmetic behind it is [in the detail](developers/status-detail.md#connection-tracking).

**Two things reach the filter, and a rule names which.** One is a conversation opening. The other is
traffic an existing conversation is the *reason* for without belonging to it — today an ICMP error
quoting one of its datagrams — which whoever sent it composed, with a source address of their
choosing. Recognising it decides where it would go and never whether it may, so it is put to the
filter like anything else and a document that admits no such traffic denies it. A rule writes
`tracking="opening"`, `tracking="related"`, or `tracking="any"`. There is deliberately no
`established` value: traffic inside a tracked conversation never reaches the filter, so the word
would name a choice an operator does not have, and it is refused rather than accepted and ignored.

It costs something, and the cost is in two places. A packet the tracker cannot keep state for is
refused *before* the filter, so no rule can permit a non-initial fragment, a protocol the appliance
does not decode, or a TCP segment from the middle of a conversation it never saw begin; each of
those refusals is its own reason on `/metrics`. There is no NAT. The ARP and ICMP that exist belong to the
management port alone — the dataplane resolves a next hop from a static neighbour table and answers
nothing for itself.

**The tracker's lifecycle reaches the recordings.** `/logs.pcapng` is a connection history: a
conversation opening names the rule that admitted it, an advance names what the packet moved, a close
names *how* it closed, a policy refusal names the rule or the default deny that refused it, a tracker
refusal names its reason, and a conversation a policy commit ended names the flow it ended and the
state it was in. Every record but that last one is anchored to the packet that caused it, so the file
opens in a pcapng reader as a packet list with real addresses and ports; the revocation is anchored to
no packet and **says so** rather than inventing one — no captured bytes, no wire length, no direction
and no classification, which is what a record about a conversation rather than about a frame looks
like. What is still missing is a **recording selector**: the capture sink records every frame the
dataplane decided on rather than the flows a policy picks out, and the filter rules above decide what
the appliance *forwards* and select nothing for recording. A conversation reclaimed by its idle
timeout still produces no close event — that record would have no causing packet either, and unlike a
revocation nothing emits one.

## Management plane redesign

The two-component target — the channel, onboarding, the store, and the management server — measured
against what exists. The [contracts](contracts/configuration-package.md) the rows below implement
against are written; onboarding, the store and the channel's own handshake and greeting now implement
their part of them, and everything the channel is supposed to *carry* does not.

| Capability | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Outbound management channel | **partial** | the persistent mutually-authenticated connection of the [channel framing contract](contracts/channel-framing.md). **The appliance now dials where it was told, and keeps dialling**: the management domain reads the endpoint the store domain published, opens an outbound session out of its own port — resolve the next hop, dial, hold the connection — and reports **one console record per attempt** whichever way that attempt went, plus the counts that place the failure and the wait before the next attempt where it did not (below). It re-dials on the contract's own schedule: bounded exponential backoff with full jitter, one second doubling to a five-minute cap, each wait drawn uniformly below the current bound from a generator seeded per boot from `RDRAND` — **a draw of its own and never the transport's sequence-number secret**, a redial instant being observable to anybody on the wire. **The schedule now genuinely starts fresh, and only on a greeting agreed with the far end**: that fact crosses back over the relay as a latching word of its own, read as a level rather than an edge so a coalesced wakeup cannot lose it, and nothing else resets the wait — a connection that merely came up buys nothing, which is the contract's rule rather than a simplification, a server that accepts and closes every connection being exactly what a reset on a completed handshake would hand a redial loop. **An appliance nobody owns has nowhere to dial**, which is a state and not a failed attempt: nothing is counted, nothing is scheduled, and it says so once on the console rather than being silent. **Management unreachability is never traffic-affecting** — the dataplane forwards the last committed configuration however long the channel is down, and nothing about the channel's state gates it, the two domains sharing no region that carries it. A booted release image is held to the whole of that on both sides of the wire — and to how it **fails**, on four more boots whose station misbehaves in each of the four ways a management server or the link to it can: each is held to its **first** attempt, which reports the one outcome that misbehaviour produces, and the node goes on forwarding and counting throughout. **Every one of those failures is now diagnosable from the console alone**, which is the only debug surface a deployed node has: each distinct cause has a token of its own — a dead server, one refusing the port and one that is not speaking TCP correctly were a single token between them, and are three now — and an attempt that failed adds four further records carrying the facts that place it and the wait before the next one, five where the station acknowledged a number that was never sent. **And the TLS client over that dial now runs, in the domain that holds the keys.** The cryptography domain drives it over the outbound byte stream the management domain provides, across the relay those two domains already shared — **no new region, no new channel and no new send capability**, the relay that carries an administrator's onboarding session inbound being the same shape carrying this one outbound. It is the onboarding server's sibling and the same shape — bytes in a delivery at a time, bytes back into the room the wire has, the session held across calls — and it is where this appliance's two trust decisions meet: it presents the device certificate a management authority issued and signs through the same key delegation the boot proof uses, and it validates the server against **the delivered anchor and nothing else** — no system roots, no other authority — holding that certificate to the address literal it dialled rather than to a name, so no resolver enters the decision. It terminates the record layer alone. **The framing runs over it too**: the whole wire protocol of the contract as a codec — the eight-byte header, the ten frames in both directions, both greetings and the server's two resume cursors — and deliberately no decision about *when* a frame is sent, which is the session's above it. A frame carries up to a mebibyte and arrives in record-sized pieces, so the decoder is incremental and reassembles into a buffer the **caller** owns, exactly sized by its type and holding one frame's worth and never two; and **nothing behind a header this end will refuse is ever taken**, so a length past the bound, an unknown type, a nonzero reserved byte, a frame from the wrong end or a document past its own bound each cost eight bytes rather than a mebibyte of buffering. **Thirteen refusals, one per rule broken**, because a deployed appliance is diagnosed from its console alone; a violation closes the connection and never panics. It is held to literal wire bytes, round-tripped a byte at a time in both directions, and fuzzed with arbitrary streams cut at arbitrary points — where every frame decoded must re-encode to exactly the bytes it came from. **How a handshake ended is a value with one variant per cause**, so the console names each without a token standing for three: the ten the server end has, plus the two only this end can meet — the delivered anchor refusing the server's certificate, carrying *which way* it refused, and an anchor no verifier can be built over at all, which is a fault in what was installed rather than in what the peer presented. **Which end judged what** is what the vocabulary keeps apart: this appliance's own verdict on the server is the certificate variant, and the server's verdict on this appliance arrives as the fatal alert it sent and in no other form, because TLS 1.3 gives a client no message saying its certificate was accepted — which is also why a completed handshake is reported only once the peer has spoken on it, so an appliance the management server refuses is never reported as a channel that came up. It is host-tested against a real rustls server — a whole mutually-authenticated handshake and an application-data round trip through the very interface the domain uses, plus one driver per failure arm — and fuzzed with arbitrary streams cut into arbitrary deliveries over an anchor and a certificate the input chooses. **And a booted release image now establishes a real one against a server this project did not write.** A scenario dials an `openssl s_server` through QEMU's user-mode stack — which turns the appliance's connection to its gateway into a connection to the host's loopback, so nothing about the appliance's own addressing is special-cased — and the boot is judged from **both** ends: the appliance must report TLS 1.3 with `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256` and the hybrid post-quantum group `X25519MLKEM768`, presenting the device certificate that run's own development authority issued, and the server must record that it validated a client certificate whose subject is the identifier the store domain printed on that same boot and whose chain reaches that authority and no other. **Both greetings cross**: this end's is found in the server's transcript as the literal wire bytes the contract fixes, the server's is read back off the record layer, and the console says a version was agreed with one frame each way. **Four scenarios judge the channel**, and the other three are the three distinct ways it does not come up, each under a token of its own — nothing listening at all, a server the delivered anchor refuses by name, and a server that will not have this appliance, which arrives as the alert it sent with **no** established record beside it, that refusal happening inside the handshake before a byte crosses under the traffic keys. **Which half a session is now crosses the relay as a stated fact rather than being worked out at each end.** An open names it — onboarding, where an administrator dialled in, or channel, where the appliance dialled out — decided by the domain that owns the network, that being the only end which knows what took the connection, and pinned for the session's life. Each end used to infer it from a predicate of its own, and that was wrong in both directions. **A session says how it ended as well as how it came up**, on a second console record and never a third: the outcome the handshake settled on, and — only where that was an established session — the one thing that then ended it, whether a fatal alert from a peer that had already spoken, a clean goodbye, a flood, or an exhausted arena. The bound is two by construction and first-party, so a server that abuses a session a thousand times over leaves the same two lines as one that abuses it once. **Above the framing the greeting and the two upstream recording frames are implemented, and a frame that is not the greeting is counted and dropped** — deliberately not a violation: a server that speaks the rest of the protocol to an appliance which has not shipped its half is a server running ahead of this build, and refusing it would make an upgrade of one end an outage of the pair. **And the recording rings now go up it.** The domain that owns the network reads its own cursor into each ring out of the recorder over the download channel the HTTP surface already used — in a coordinate of its own, the ring's absolute append position rather than the download's per-snapshot offset, because that offset is resolved against an origin recomputed at every boot and a cursor a server holds across one could not survive it — and hands the recording, the position and the bytes over the relay as semantic fields. The frame is composed at the far end, where the framing's vocabulary lives: had a length been stated by the domain that owns the network, a defect there would print on this appliance's own console as *the management server* breaking the protocol and send an operator to the far end of a wire they cannot reach. What that does not buy is honesty about content, which nothing at this layer can — that domain reads the medium's window — and what it does buy is that the frame **type** is the composing end's alone. **One whole frame per relay item and never a fragment**: the bound is not the item, which holds a maximal TLS record, but the single answer buffer the terminating end has, so the arithmetic runs backwards from 4096 — a record's 22 bytes of overhead, eight of frame header and eight of ring position leave 4058 ring bytes — and the encoder is all-or-nothing into that room, so no half of a length-prefixed frame is ever on the wire and no domain holds partial-frame state. What makes a small frame whole information rather than a fragment is the position it carries: an upstream frame is self-locating at any size. **What arrives is always handed over before anything is shipped**, the window being one item and inbound bytes carrying the acknowledgements a cursor will move on, and **an operator's download wins outright** over the channel's cursor, being bounded and waited on where the cursor loses nothing by standing still. The cursor moves on the **answer** and never on the issue, so a shipment that was refused, faulted or never answered is re-shipped rather than skipped — a frame may cross twice and never be lost, which is what the position makes harmless. A booted release image is held to it from both ends: the appliance's console must say its frame tally passed the greeting, and the `openssl s_server` at the far end must receive a well-formed `UP_RECORDS` frame from ring position 0 whose bytes open on a pcapng Section Header Block. **Missing:** the server's acknowledgement cursor, which is decoded and dropped rather than advancing anything, and the reader cursor in the ring superblock — so a reboot re-ships from the beginning. **Overrun is not resynchronised**: a ring the traffic wrapped past while the cursor stood in it stops being shipped for the rest of the boot, under a console token of its own per recording, rather than continuing from bytes that are not the ones the position names. No configuration travels the channel, and what the framing still leaves to a session is the flush cadence and the commit-confirm over a fresh connection. No metric counts a framing violation either, and no scenario drives one: the four boots point a real server or a deliberate silence at the appliance, and a peer that breaks the framing is one `openssl s_server` cannot be made to play. **Both of the two values that client needs reach the domains that use them.** Where to dial is one word the store domain publishes into a region of its own — the address and the port packed into a single naturally aligned load, so the pair cannot be read half-written — which **two** domains now map read-only and no third maps at all: not the configuration domain, so where this appliance reports to cannot become a value composed by the parser that reads an attacker's document, and not the forwarder, so a compromised dataplane cannot tell an operator's session from the traffic around it. **The second reader is the one added with this client, and it is a trust decision rather than a convenience**: the domain that *validates* reads the address itself, because a server's certificate is held to the address literal that was dialled and to no name, so an address handed over by the domain that owns the network would let that domain point the validation at a name a server it controls holds a certificate for — and the relay's own ABI has no field for one either. The write grant stays the store domain's alone and is withheld from both readers. A read there confers an address literal and a port and nothing else, both of which the management server publishes to every appliance it owns. Whom to believe crosses the **key delegation** as a fourth answer beside the certificate, the public key and the ownership fact, because that is the domain that validates the server's certificate and the record holding the anchor is the record that channel already answers out of; it cost that channel's reply region 768 bytes of the 3150 it had spare, and no grant moved. Both are fail-closed by construction rather than by convention: an appliance nobody has taken publishes an untagged word that reads as **nowhere** rather than as `0.0.0.0`, and is refused an anchor **by name** rather than handed zero bytes it might read as one. Both readings are on the console — the store domain says where it told the channel to dial and whether it told it anywhere, the cryptography domain says whether it was handed an authority and how large it is, and the gate holds that word to the store domain's own `onboarded=` on the same boot, because neither domain can check itself. **Both are now used for what they were delivered for:** the management domain reads that word and dials what it names, and the cryptography domain builds its verifier over that anchor and judges the server the dial reached against it — so an appliance reaches the address its owner installed and speaks to it under the authority its owner delivered |
| Onboarding | **partial** | the HTTPS onboarding server, the CSR, the package upload and its tar reader ([contract](contracts/configuration-package.md)). **The port and the handover under it now do.** The management endpoint listens on a second TCP port of its own — not the plain-HTTP one — and carries a byte stream on it rather than a request and a response: one connection at a time, fixed arrays each way, and nothing in the crate that owns the network that interprets a byte. What arrives crosses to the domain that holds the keys over two regions with opposite grants — one connection at a time because there is no identifier in the ABI to name a second, no operation in it that asks for a plaintext, and no field in it a private key fits in — and that domain accepts the session, counts what crosses and closes. Every distinct way that path can fail has a console token of its own; both domains report each session's own account, and the close that ends a session carries **how** it ended, so a session the transport forgot is not reported as one the peer closed by the end that cannot see the wire. The port's own counts — connections accepted and lost, bytes a peer sent past its window, bytes an answer had no room for — reach the console beside each session and `/metrics` whether a session ever ended, and **the transport under it now reaches `/metrics` as well**, told apart from the HTTP server's stack by a `service` label rather than by a second set of families: a second administrator's handshake refused for want of the port's one slot used to move nothing any surface carried. An open is itself the end of any session the far end still believed in, so an answer this port gave up on costs one session rather than every session after it. **A booted release image is now held to that port on three boots**, one per way a session on it can end: a session an administrator finishes with, one a peer abandons by resetting after the appliance has acknowledged its payload, and a second connection opened while one is established — which an established connection is never given up for, so the appliance answers it with nothing at all and the boot carries one session rather than two. Each holds both domains' account of the one session to each other and to what the station on the wire actually sent, and the node goes on forwarding and counting to the byte throughout. **And the far end of that handover now terminates TLS.** The cryptography domain opens a TLS 1.3 server when the relay opens a session, drives it a delivery at a time and answers with what it produced, presents the appliance's own onboarding certificate taken from the domain that minted it, signs through the same key delegation the boot proof uses, and winds its bounded arena back at both ends of every session — so a peer that opens a thousand connections costs the region what one costs. **How each handshake ended reaches the console with the facts that ending holds**, which is the whole point: one token per cause and none standing for two — completed with its three code points, a peer that sent nothing, a peer that left part way, the alert a peer sent, the library's own incompatibility, a suite or group mismatch carrying what the peer really offered, this end's own refusal as the library's error variant, an exhausted arena, a direction that outgrew what it holds — with the library's two error not write**: `openssl s_client` completes a handshake over `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256` and `X25519MLKEM768` and is shown a certificate whose subject is the identifier the store domain printed on the same boot, and three more clients — one speaking only TLS 1.2, one offering a suite this appliance does not have, and one that connects and says nothing — each draw their own token. **And the read-only half of the onboarding protocol now runs above that record layer.** The session carries HTTP/1.1, and it serves exactly two things: `GET /` is a deliberately plain, unstyled page carrying the appliance's identifier and the SPKI fingerprint an administrator compares against the console, rendered exactly as the [certificate profile](contracts/certificate-profile.md) fixes it; and `GET /certificate.csr` is the PKCS#10 request that profile defines, subject common name the device identifier, **signed through the same key delegation** and composed **once at bring-up** rather than per request — so what an unauthenticated peer can provoke by asking for it is a copy of an array and never a signature in the domain that holds the key. Everything else is refused, each way under a console token of its own: the rate limiter, an identity that does not exist yet, an address nothing serves, a method nothing serves it under, a head that never ends, and the fifteen the request parser distinguishes. The endpoints are **rate-limited with backoff and never permanently locked out** — a burst of eight, an interval that doubles per consecutive refusal up to thirty-two seconds and no further, and a wait that always expires, because a lockout that did not would be a way to make an unonboarded appliance unonboardable from across a network. A fifth boot proves it with `curl`: five requests over one boot, every one pinned to the fingerprint the store domain printed on that same boot, the page holding that fingerprint and that identifier, the request read back through `openssl req` as a PKCS#10 whose subject is that identifier with its own signature verified, and three requests refused under three different tokens. **And the package an administrator carries back is now read**, though nothing yet takes delivery of one: a reader holds an uploaded archive to the whole of the [package contract](contracts/configuration-package.md) — uncompressed ustar only, the magic and version required, every GNU and PAX extension refused by its own type flag, regular files only, the four names compared byte for byte with no path prefix, every size field read as bounded octal and checked against the bytes present, every header checksum verified, exactly four members each exactly once — and then holds what they carry to its rules: the device certificate must bind **this appliance's own key**, taken from the one place in the certificate that binds it rather than found somewhere in its bytes; the endpoint must be an address literal and a port; and the document goes through the same configuration reader a document submitted any other way does. Whether the certificate chains to the delivered anchor is **not** decided there: it is an injected verifier the cryptography domain will supply out of the chain validator it already carries, and a package cannot be produced without that verifier having accepted — the type says so, not a comment. Nothing partial ever leaves: there is no way to hold a package that did not pass every rule. It is proved against a package **the management server itself produced**, committed as a fixture, and the server's own suite asserts its writer still reproduces that fixture byte for byte — so the two implementations of one format cannot drift apart without one of the two gates failing. **And the appliance now takes ownership out of one.** The domain that holds the device key answers a fourth delegation operation: the archive crosses in a dedicated 128 KiB staging region the cryptography domain writes and the store domain reads, the request states how many bytes of it there are, and the answer is a status word — installed, or refused. The store snapshots that region into its own staging window before it validates, so a package is judged on bytes its writer can no longer reach; it re-applies every structural rule, compares the device certificate against **the key in its own state record** rather than anything a peer offers, and verifies **one signature under one profile** — one algorithm, one curve, a path of length one — which is deliberately narrower than the chain validation the cryptography domain will run and is the whole reason two readings are worth having. What passes is written into the A/B state record and flushed, and only then does the console carry the anchor's fingerprint and the endpoint. Every distinct rule that can refuse a package has a console token of its own — one hundred and nine of them — and one boot serves eight installs, because each costs an archive walk and a signature a peer paces. **And the writing half of that surface now runs too, so an appliance can be given an owner over the wire.** `POST /configuration.tar` takes the package under one framing and no other — a single decimal `Content-Length`, bounded at the archive's own 128 KiB — and **the body never rests in the surface**: it is handed on as it arrives, segment by segment, straight into the staging region the domain that holds the device key reads out of. The head buffer is filled before it is parsed, which is what lets one TLS delivery carry a head and thirty kibibytes of body without the head being called too long. The cryptography domain then copies the staged archive **back out of that region** into a 128 KiB window taken from its bounded arena — read back rather than accumulated, so the bytes it judges are the bytes the other domain installs — and holds them to the whole package contract with the chain check answered by the **certificate validator this appliance already adopted**: a client verifier over a root store holding the delivered anchor, asked whether it issued the delivered device certificate. The window is reserved against the arena's own headroom **before a byte is placed**, so an appliance with nowhere to put a package refuses the request rather than beginning one it cannot finish. What passes crosses the `Install` delegation to the store domain, which reads it a second and deliberately narrower time against its own record and writes the medium. **An accepted package shuts the surface for good**: on the spot, before the answer is composed, and every later request on every address is `410 Gone` under a token of its own rather than "no such resource" — and the close survives a reboot without a flag, because the delegation's identity answer now carries whether the record on the medium names an owner and the surface is *constructed* shut when it does. Each of the **twenty-six** distinct refusals has its own console token: the six new ones name an owned appliance, an upload declaring no body, a peer sending past the length it declared, an appliance with no room to validate one, bytes that would not all go where they were meant to, and a package the key holder judged and refused — that last carrying no reason, because which rule refused it is that domain's own record in the package contract's vocabulary, which is one catalogue both domains raise and the console chapter now lists once under both. The page prints the `curl` command rather than a form, the package being the whole body of the request and a browser only able to send one wrapped in an encoding this appliance would have to unwrap. **And onboarding now runs end to end on a booted image, with the harness playing the management server.** Two scenarios carry one appliance from unowned to owned and back through a restart: the first fetches the request over the pinned TLS an administrator uses, reads its subject back with `openssl` and holds it to the identifier the console printed, issues a device certificate against a certification authority generated for that checkout alone — under the build tree, never committed, removed by `clean` — composes a package to the [package contract](contracts/configuration-package.md), and uploads it whole. What it asserts is not a status line: the appliance must print **that authority's own SPKI fingerprint**, computed on the harness's side before the appliance says it, and **the endpoint the package named**, at a generation the install advanced, having accounted for the archive to the byte. **Two packages are refused first, each under its own token** — one well formed and certified to another appliance's key, one whose archive is not ustar — because an accepted package shuts the surface and so an install is the last decision a boot can make; after it, a fresh connection asking for the page and another offering the same package are both `410 Gone`. The second boot carries that medium into a second start: the same device under the same key, owned, with **every address on the surface gone**, the package it accepted included — which is what makes the close permanent rather than per-boot. **And an appliance nobody owns now forwards nothing.** The domain that holds the identity publishes one word into a region of its own — written at bring-up and again the instant an install commits — and the forwarding domain maps it read-only, reads it on the wakeups the frames themselves provoke, and asks it **first**, in front of admission and routing and everything else: while it does not say owned, every frame is refused under a drop reason of its own, counted per pipeline, carried into the capture with that reason on it, and named on the console. So a node that carries nothing says *why* on the three surfaces that explain every other refusal instead of being a firewall whose traffic disappears. The reading is **latched**, because the writer is a peer and one that could clear the word would hold a switch over the whole dataplane: the forwarding domain follows the one transition a boot can honestly carry — being adopted while it runs — and never the reverse, an owner being given up only by a factory reset that takes effect on the next boot. One region, one writer, one reader and **no notification**; the read is withheld from the configuration domain so ownership cannot become a table composed by the parser that reads an attacker's document, and from the management domain, which can already ask for the identity. The system gate is arranged around it for no extra boot: the scenario that onboards an appliance runs first and twenty-three scenarios boot their own copy of the medium it leaves, which is what a deployed appliance is; the ones that stay unowned are the ones whose subject that is; and every boot is held to the word its medium carried against the word the forwarding domain printed. The [cryptography profile](reference/crypto-profile.md) states all of it and is held to the target specification, the console vocabulary and the gate's own ceilings by the build. **The session's server half now authenticates under the appliance's real key, which lives in another protection domain.** The signing interface has a second implementation that forwards over a channel and returns the same bytes, so nothing above that line changed; the domain holds no scalar of any kind for that identity, and what crosses is a public point, a public name and signatures, because those are the only shapes the ABI has fields for. **And this domain now dials with it.** It runs the channel's client half over the byte stream the management domain's outbound session carries, so the appliance's own trust decisions are exercised against a peer nobody here wrote: a booted image establishes TLS 1.3 with `TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256` and `X25519MLKEM768` against a real `openssl s_server`, presenting the device certificate the harness's development authority issued and validating the server against the delivered anchor and nothing else. The boot's own self-session stays beside it, because what that one proves is the provider and the vector suite rather than interoperability. **And it now composes the appliance's upstream frames.** The domain that owns the network hands it a recording, a ring position and the ring's own bytes; this domain puts the header on and pushes the whole frame into the record layer, refusing — under three tokens of its own, each ending the session — a shipment longer than one frame may carry, one offered before the greeting is agreed, and one a session already holding too much on its way out could not take whole. It refuses rather than dropping because the cursor at the other end moves on the answer, so a shipment that vanished quietly would be a gap nothing could notice. **Missing:** no session this domain terminates has yet carried a range read or a configuration document, and the ending a session that came up reports is host-tested rather than observed on a booted node, which is a recorded deviation with a reason rather than an oversight. SHA-NI is not reachable on this target, for a reason the detail chapter records. Per-primitive cost comes off the image on every boot; what remains unmeasured is the whole-path throughput target, which needs bare metal and an external traffic generator rather than a guest |
| Persistent store | **partial** | the third virtio-blk device and the store domain exist, and the [double-buffered state record](design/configuration.md#persistence) is written and read back: two 4 KiB copies with a monotonic generation and a SHA-256 digest each, a whole-state write into one copy and a device flush behind it, so a power cut costs the newer copy and leaves the older decodable. `VIRTIO_BLK_F_FLUSH` is now negotiated on both block devices and the store's own commit waits for the flush to complete, as does the write that clears a [factory-reset](design/updates.md#factory-reset) request before the reset destroys anything. **Missing:** the configuration history — the slot array is in the record and nothing places a document in it — and a reset of the recorder's medium, which needs a layout decision rather than a repeat of the store's |
| Transport active open | **partial** | the TCP stack now opens connections: `SynSent`, a connect entry point, the simultaneous open, RFC 793's reset-not-a-state-change answer to an acknowledgement of something never sent, and an unanswered dial abandoned in silence at the retransmission limit. A neighbour cache exists beside it — bounded, expiring, learning only replies it asked for, and refusing to re-bind a resolved entry — held by unit, property and fuzz tests. Two of the three things dialling out needs are now built and unused: an **ARP request writer**, whose type cannot express a request addressed to a station this end has not resolved, and a **route decision** that answers on-link with the destination and off-link with the port's gateway, refusing anything else under its own typed cause and never handing back an address that is not a unicast station on this port's own link. The **configuration document can now state that gateway**: `<management>` carries one — an address, or the word `none` for a port that reaches only its own link — refused at load time if it is not unicast, is the port's own address, or lies off the port's prefix, and re-decided on the same three rules where it crosses into the handover image. It sits on `<management>` and not on `<interface>` because the management port is the only one that dials; a gateway on a dataplane interface would be a value nothing in this build reads. The **endpoint now uses all three**: it holds the gateway the committed document states, chooses a next hop with the route decision, asks for that next hop's hardware address with an ARP request, takes the reply into the cache under the cache's own three rules, and drives an outbound session — open to an address and port, then move bytes each way for as long as the connection lasts. A segment for a next hop that is not resolved yet is dropped under a typed reason rather than queued, the transport's own retransmission being what re-sends it, and the return path a dial uses comes from the resolution rather than from the frames its answer arrives in, so whoever answers cannot redirect what this end sends next. A session gives its connection back to the transport when it ends, whichever way it ended, so the next dial to the same address and port opens a genuinely new one — and what that release owes the peer follows from the state the connection stood in rather than from the call. **The management domain is now the caller.** Once its port is addressed it opens a session to the endpoint its store published, drives it on every wakeup, and reports the outcome — established, or the typed reason the attempt ended — in one console record **per attempt**, with the counts that place a failure and the wait before the next attempt in further records beside it. Nothing about an attempt is bounded by elapsed time, so a slow station is a slow station; what elapsed time decides is only the wait *between* attempts, on the channel contract's own backoff. A booted release image proves it from both sides at once: the appliance's own record, and a station on the wire that saw the resolution, answered it, accepted the connection, read the probe and answered it. **And the adversarial cases are now proved on a booted node too**, one boot each, against a station that answers the resolution and never the connection, one that resets it, one that answers a `SYN` by acknowledging a number that was never sent, and one that answers for an address nobody asked about. Each is held to its first attempt, which reports the one outcome its misbehaviour can produce — and in each the node stays healthy: the dataplane goes on forwarding through the whole boot, the management port reports every frame put on its wire to the byte, and neither the appliance's retransmission budget nor the floor its schedule puts between two attempts is exceeded. The neighbour cache's eight counters and the outbound half's nine now reach `/metrics` beside the transport's. The dial's own vocabulary is 13 outcomes, one per distinct cause, and a failing attempt reports beside it where its frames were sent and which part of the addressing chose that, how many requests the resolution spent and what it learned, how many replies the port turned away and why, how many handshakes went out, whether anything came back at all, the resets in each direction, how long until the next attempt and the bound that wait was drawn below, and — where a station claimed a sequence number that was never sent — that number against the one really sent. **And the channel's TLS session is now the consumer of that stream**, reached over the relay this domain already held for the onboarding port, so a connection that comes up carries a mutually-authenticated session and the channel's greeting rather than being held silent — and the open that starts a session now states **which half** it is, so the terminating end answers an outbound connection with a client and an inbound one with a server instead of inferring it from a predicate of its own. **And that stream is now a sliding window rather than a budget for the session**: what the peer acknowledges is released and the room reused, keyed on the transport's own oldest unacknowledged number so a byte is given up only once no retransmission can ask for it again, and a window that is momentarily full is backpressure — the relay issues no item until the wire can take a whole answer — rather than a refusal that ends the channel. A session may therefore carry unboundedly many bytes through a fixed array, which is what streaming a recording ring over it will need. **Missing:** the stream is one connection at a time in each direction, the relay's ABI having no identifier for a second, so nothing here is exercised against a management plane that opens two |
| Configuration over the channel | **open** | stage, validate, commit with confirmation over a fresh connection, rollback, and the version history — the operations exist today only as `POST /config`'s single stage-validate-commit step |
| Management server (`ctrld`) | **partial** | an administrator can sign in and onboard an appliance end to end: the server is its own certificate authority with its signing keys sealed in Postgres under an environment-supplied key, it validates an uploaded PKCS#10 request against the [certificate profile](contracts/certificate-profile.md), issues a device certificate, and hands back an onboarding [package](contracts/configuration-package.md) whose bytes `tar` and `openssl` verify. Local accounts, an inventory with an honest status, and an audit trail exist; the ClickHouse telemetry schema and its writer exist with no producer. **And the channel now has a listener.** It serves the endpoint certificate on the endpoint's own port, requires and verifies a client certificate against this server's authority and nothing else, identifies the appliance from that verified certificate rather than from anything it says, exchanges greetings, and reads the contract's frames — so **online, offline and last seen are now facts the inventory holds**, derived from a live session and the instant one last ended, and announced on PubSub as they change. What arrives is bounded and refused by name and never with a crash: twelve refusals for the framing, one per rule broken and read in the order the appliance's own codec reads them, and five for a certificate that does not name an appliance this server holds — with a deadline on the TLS handshake and an **absolute** one on the greeting, so a peer that dribbles bytes runs the same clock down as one that says nothing. **And the two ends now share a key exchange, so an appliance can reach it.** The listener offers the hybrid `X25519MLKEM768` and nothing beside it, which is exactly what the appliance offers: the runtime runs an Erlang/OTP whose `:ssl` implements that group, on a base whose OpenSSL provides the ML-KEM under it — the base being the half that does not expire as the runtime advances, since `:crypto` takes ML-KEM from the OpenSSL it links rather than implementing it, and the group is matched rather than widened — a classical group offered here as well would be one any peer reaching the port could choose, giving up the harvest-now-decrypt-later property on a channel carrying a customer's network history. A hybrid-only client completes the handshake, exchanges greetings and is refused nothing; a client offering only the classical half is refused `insufficient security` before either certificate is examined, which is the assertion that holds the offer to one group. Both are in the gate. **The two implementations have still never met**, which is the gap that remains: each end is held to a peer the other did not write — this listener to an `:ssl` client offering the appliance's own group and suite, the appliance to the `openssl s_server` a booted release image dials — and no boot yet points an appliance at this server, so nothing has held one session to both readings of the framing and both certificate profiles at once. **And the recordings it receives can now be read**: a streaming pcapng decoder takes the ring bytes in whatever pieces a transport delivers them, learns each section's byte order from its own header, and answers the blocks the appliance writes — the section, its interfaces, every record with its flow, rule and event annotation decoded, and the custom block that seals a segment — or one of twenty-one named refusals, never a raise. It is held to three recordings the appliance's own encoder wrote, committed as fixtures, and to yielding the identical blocks from those bytes cut at every offset there is. **Nothing yet folds a decoded record into the telemetry schema**, so ring bytes stop at a documented seam and ClickHouse still has no producer; and the web interface is plain HTTP — [detail](developers/status-detail.md#management-server) |

## Open decisions and known risks

The design chapters record the settled target picture. The following are, respectively, decisions
not yet made and known risks. They are recorded here so they are not mistaken for oversights.

### Open decisions

- **CA signing-trust sharing across HA nodes** — required; the form (e.g. per-node intermediate CAs
  under a common trusted root) is open. This is the TLS-*interception* CA; the device-issuing CA is
  settled — it is the [management server](design/management-server.md).
- **CA rollover for the management CA.** The [non-remotable trust anchor](design/threat-model.md#the-compromised-management-server)
  makes remote anchor rotation impossible, so rotating the management CA means visiting every
  appliance. Two directions exist — carrying two anchor slots and permitting the *addition* of a
  second while the current one is valid (reversible, therefore not self-harming), never the removal
  of the last; or deferring entirely. Deferred, because there is no fleet yet; this is the one item
  that control is expected to force a revisit of.
- **HA-link split-brain arbitration** — witness, quorum, or fencing.
- **Trusted time source mechanism** for the inspection path. The management channel deliberately
  judges its certificates against the CMOS-derived clock (a recorded decision in the
  [threat model](design/threat-model.md)); validating upstream certificates on behalf of protected
  clients still needs a genuinely trusted source, and that mechanism is open.
- **Proxy vs. cut-through throughput split** — the proportion of traffic on the terminating proxy
  path, which drives core sizing.
- **Access control and multi-admin concurrent editing in the management server** — roles and scopes
  over appliances and policy subtrees, and the concurrent-editing model (changeset review with
  semantic merge, or a live shared candidate with presence).
- **Whether anything replaces CI.** The pipeline definition is deleted and the git hooks are the
  whole gate; no replacement has been decided.

### Known risks

- **x86_64 seL4/Microkit maturity.** x86_64 on Microkit is recent (added in 2.1.0, November 2025)
  and exposes only generic/QEMU platforms (no dedicated x86 hardware board); x86_64 with
  SMP is the least-exercised seL4 configuration.
- **No existing 10 Gbit/s or x86 NIC driver.** The public sDDF tree contains only virtio and
  Arm-SoC drivers; all NIC drivers (virtio, SFP+ 10G, netvsc, MANA) are implemented from scratch in
  Rust.
- **TCP stack effort.** Building a first-party proxy TCP stack is a substantial effort, and it
  is larger than the one an adopted stack would have left: the correctness of a transport under
  hostile input, and the years of exposure that establishes it, are what adopting one buys and
  writing one forgoes. SACK, congestion control, and scaling to many concurrent proxied connections
  at 10 Gbit/s are each ahead. The trade is deliberate — that effort against a copy per segment —
  and naming it does not reduce it.
- **Pure-Rust signature matching at line rate.** Whether the pure-Rust `aho-corasick` /
  `regex-automata` engines sustain line rate with a realistic ruleset is unproven and needs an
  early benchmark (as does the crypto provider).
- **Azure platform scope.** Azure support requires Hyper-V/VMBus (for netvsc), the MANA driver,
  Gateway Load Balancer VXLAN handling, and seL4 booting as an Azure guest — a substantial platform
  effort, not a single NIC driver.
- **SHA-NI is unreachable on this target, and the same mechanism is what keeps AVX out.** The
  adopted hash library selects its SHA-NI backend through a runtime feature probe that compiles to
  a constant `false` on a freestanding target, so SHA-256 runs the portable implementation whatever
  the processor offers. Making that probe run would also make the AVX probes in the cipher
  libraries run, and those must not: the pinned kernel does not save the wide vector state, so an
  AVX instruction in a protection domain is silent corruption rather than a fault. The build now
  asserts the absence by disassembling the shipped domains — both the wide registers and, since the
  BMI2 withdrawal below, the vector encoding itself. Recovering SHA-NI without recovering
  AVX needs either a per-crate way to force the backend or a wider kernel save area, and neither is
  in scope; the cost is one primitive running at roughly a tenth of its accelerated rate, measured
  and reported on every boot.
- **BMI2 was withdrawn from the SIMD target, and the image is now proved on both accelerators.**
  The three-tier hardware profile placed ADX and BMI2 in a "free" tier on the reasoning that
  general-purpose-register instructions carry no saved vector state. That is true of the register
  file and false of what matters: BMI2 is **VEX-encoded**, and an emulated processor refuses a
  VEX-encoded instruction — the general-purpose ones included — unless the guest has *enabled* the
  vector state, which a kernel saving only x87 and SSE state never does. Real parts do not enforce
  that, so the image ran under KVM and took an invalid-opcode fault under emulation, inside the
  P-256 scalar multiplication, on a guest whose `CPUID` advertised BMI2 and whose feature gate had
  passed. An acceleration only one of the two accelerators can execute is one the gate cannot
  prove, and a verdict that depends on the machine it ran on is not a verdict — so BMI2 is
  disabled, the guest CPU model no longer advertises it, and neither domain's `CPUID` gate demands
  it. ADX stays: it is legacy-encoded and unaffected, though this build currently emits no `adcx`
  or `adox` instruction, so the requirement is presently a permission rather than a use. The cost
  is a few per cent on X25519 and ML-KEM-768 and nothing measurable on ECDSA P-256, reported on the
  [cryptography profile](reference/crypto-profile.md). The build now refuses the whole encoding
  rather than the feature: it reads the raw bytes of every instruction in the shipped protection
  domains and fails on any VEX or EVEX prefix, which cannot go stale the way a list of mnemonics
  would. And what keeps "proved on both" true is a boot rather than a check: one scenario is forced
  onto the emulator whatever the machine offers, and judges this domain there.
- **The physical address window is measured, and the store domain has now spent from it.**
  Nineteen regions carry a fixed physical address inside RAM, claiming 1092 KiB and packed into
  `0x30000000..0x3110D000` — so RAM must reach past **785.05 MiB**, and under the harness's 1 GiB it
  does with **238.95 MiB** to spare; the upper bound is unchanged, RAM having to stay below the
  `0x50000000` device window at 1280 MiB. The two regions added are the store device's virtqueue
  page and its staging window — 4 KiB and 256 KiB, the second being `lfw_blk`'s grant for the block
  device class rather than this domain's appetite, whose whole record is eight sectors. Its
  configuration page and relocated BAR sit outside RAM in the ECAM and PCI-hole windows, which are
  nowhere near full. **None of the cryptography domain's four regions is in that count**, the arena included — now 4 MiB,
  having grown by two with the channel's framing: nothing reads them by physical address, so they are
  ordinary untyped memory. The
  window is not what constrains the next phase; it is still unasserted, and that is unchanged.
- **The post-quantum primitive is RustCrypto `ml-kem`, and the architecture chapter now names it.**
  `libcrux-ml-kem` — formally verified, and the first choice while it was still a hypothesis — was
  investigated and **does build** cleanly for this target, portable path and all. What it costs is
  the dependency policy: a transitive crate of its own takes an unconditional dependency on a
  random-number crate a major version ahead of the one the elliptic-curve crates here already use,
  which puts two versions of it in the graph — a duplicate the policy denies — and it pulls a libc
  binding into an appliance that has no libc. RustCrypto `ml-kem` is audited, FIPS 203 final, and
  costs no exception at all, so it is what is adopted. The formal-verification assurance is the
  price paid, and recovering it needs upstream to loosen that dependency.
- **The TLS session is proved against itself, which is the strongest proof available before there
  is a network.** Both ends of the handshake are in the cryptography domain, over a transport that
  is two buffers, so what is proved is the whole stack — hybrid key exchange, signature, chain
  validation, key schedule, record layer, mutual authentication, clean close. What is *not* proved
  is anything about interoperating with a peer that is not this build: a second implementation on
  the other end is the first thing the channel milestone establishes.
- **Hardware cryptography's foundational hypothesis is verified; two narrower ones remain.** The
  pinned kernel saves x87 and SSE state per thread, so the XMM instruction sets the
  [CPU baseline](design/architecture.md#hardware-cryptography-profile) requires — AES-NI,
  PCLMULQDQ, SHA-NI — are architecturally available; what disabled them was a generated default in
  the shipped target specifications. The hardware-probe protection domain — built with a
  first-party hardfloat, SSE-enabled target — now proves on every console-judged QEMU boot that
  such a domain builds and boots on Microkit, that `AESENC` and `PCLMULQDQ` execute and answer
  their known answers, and that a live XMM value survives the kernel's context switches. Still
  hypotheses, to resolve in the crypto milestone: whether the IPC fastpath preserves XMM across a
  *domain boundary* (the probe holds no channel, so its preemptions exercise the context switch and
  not the fastpath), and whether a domain can be opted out of paying for FPU state it does not use.
  AVX and AVX2 are genuinely unavailable without building the kernel ourselves, and are deferred —
  and BMI2 has joined them there, for the encoding reason recorded above rather than for the saved
  state.
- **Full-rate capture of everything is not reachable, and the recording selector is the sizing
  control.** A capture sink recording all traffic at the target rate (see the
  [recording design](design/recording.md)) would have to sustain writes at the dataplane's own
  rate, which no practical device does and which would consume the medium's write endurance in
  short order. The sink is therefore sized by the selector that decides what reaches it — not by
  the filter rules, which decide what is forwarded — and a selector drawn too broadly yields loss. The loss is reported in-band and is measurable rather than silent,
  which is the mitigation available — but it remains loss, and choosing a selector narrow enough is
  an operational discipline the appliance can measure and cannot impose.
- **The throughput target and the recording sinks compete for one memory-bandwidth budget.**
  Copying frames into a ring and writing them out draws on the same bandwidth the inspection path
  is already sized to consume. How much recording the target rate can carry is unmeasured, and
  it is the class of cost that does not appear until both run at once — so it belongs to the same
  early benchmarking as the crypto provider and the signature engines.
